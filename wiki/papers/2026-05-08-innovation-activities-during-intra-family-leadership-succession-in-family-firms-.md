# Innovation activities during intra-family leadership succession in family firms: An empirical study from a socioemotional wealth perspective

# Innovation activities during intra-family leadership succession in family firms: An empirical study from a socioemotional wealth perspective

## 1. Core Elements

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Research Question | How are socioemotional wealth (SEW) factors related to owner-managers' perceptions of the intra-family leadership succession phase as a suitable time for innovation activities in family firms? |
| Core Finding | Socioemotional factors have both "bright" and "dark" sides: family adaptability and closeness to the firm are positively associated with perceiving succession as an innovation opportunity, while intergenerational authority and history of family bonds are negatively associated with this perception. |
| Marginal Contribution | Addresses a gap in the family firm innovation literature by focusing specifically on the succession phase as a distinct time frame; moves beyond simple ownership/management proxies for SEW by measuring multiple socioemotional dimensions directly; contributes to the "Ability and Willingness Paradox" framework by showing that different non-economic factors have opposing effects on willingness to innovate. |

## 2. Economic Story

> **Motivation:** Innovation is critical for the long-term survival of family firms, yet the literature on family firm innovation remains inconsistent — some studies portray family firms as conservative and change-resistant, while others highlight their innovative capacity. The intra-family leadership succession phase is a particularly important but understudied context, as it represents a moment of potential renewal where the incumbent owner-manager's willingness to support innovation may be decisive.
>
> **Hypothesis:** Drawing on the socioemotional wealth (SEW) framework and the "Ability and Willingness Paradox" (De Massis et al., 2014), the authors argue that different socioemotional dimensions — such as family adaptability, intergenerational authority, closeness to the firm, and history of family bonds — will have divergent effects on whether owner-managers perceive the succession phase as an opportunity for innovation. Some SEW dimensions may encourage innovation (bright side), while others may inhibit it (dark side).
>
> **Finding:** Using survey data from Austrian tourism family firms, the authors find evidence consistent with their dual-sided hypothesis: family adaptability and a family member's closeness to the firm are positively associated with perceiving succession as an innovation opportunity, whereas intergenerational authority and history of family bonds are negatively associated. This suggests that the heterogeneity in family firm innovation behavior can be partly explained by variation in socioemotional factors, even when ownership structure and management control are held constant.

## 3. Preliminary Assessment

This paper may be useful for researchers studying the intersection of family firm governance, succession, and innovation. The research design — a cross-sectional survey within a single industry (Austrian tourism) — offers the advantage of holding industry context and family ownership/management influence relatively constant, which helps isolate the role of socioemotional factors. However, the correlational nature of the survey design limits causal claims; the findings describe associations between SEW dimensions and innovation perceptions rather than establishing causal mechanisms. The single-industry, single-country focus (Austrian tourism) raises questions about generalizability to other sectors and institutional contexts. The paper provides a useful conceptual framework for thinking about the "dark and bright sides" of SEW during succession, and may serve as a starting point for researchers seeking to design more causally rigorous studies on this topic.

## 4. Empirical Design

### 4.1 Data and Variables

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Data Source | Quantitative survey of owner-managers of family firms in the Austrian tourism industry. The setting was chosen to hold constant family influence (ownership and management) and major contextual variables. Exact sample size and survey year not specified in the available text, but the paper was received December 2013. |
| Y Variable | Owner-manager's perception of the intra-family leadership succession phase as a suitable/opportune time frame for innovation activities (a measure of "willingness to innovate" during succession). Measured via survey items. |
| X Variable | Socioemotional wealth (SEW) dimensions, including: (1) family adaptability, (2) closeness of a family member to the firm, (3) intergenerational authority, (4) history of family bonds. Measured via validated survey scales. |
| Key Controls | Family ownership share, family management involvement, and other major contextual variables held constant by research design (single industry: tourism; single country: Austria). |
| Sample Construction | Owner-managers of family firms in the Austrian tourism sector. The narrow definition of family firm from Chua et al. (1999) is applied. Specific inclusion/exclusion criteria and winsorization procedures are not detailed in the available text. |

### 4.2 Identification Strategy

- **Model specification:** The paper employs cross-sectional regression analysis (likely OLS or ordered logit, given the survey-based dependent variable) with the perception of succession as an innovation opportunity as the dependent variable and multiple SEW dimensions as independent variables. The general form is:

$$Y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 \text{Adaptability}_i + \beta_2 \text{Closeness}_i + \beta_3 \text{Authority}_i + \beta_4 \text{History}_i + \gamma X_i + \varepsilon_i$$

where $Y_i$ is the perception measure for firm $i$, the $\beta$ coefficients capture SEW dimensions, and $X_i$ includes controls.

- **Identifying assumption:** The paper does not claim a causal identification strategy in the econometric sense. The research design relies on the assumption that by restricting the sample to a single industry and holding family ownership/management constant, the observed associations between SEW dimensions and innovation perceptions are not driven by omitted industry-level or ownership-level confounders. This is a design-based control strategy rather than an exogenous variation strategy.

- **How is the assumption supported?** The authors argue that the single-industry (Austrian tourism) setting controls for major contextual heterogeneity. Holding family influence via ownership and management constant in the research design helps isolate the role of socioemotional (non-economic) factors. However, no formal tests for omitted variable bias or endogeneity are described in the available text.

- **Key coefficient of interest:** The $\beta$ coefficients on each SEW dimension. The authors expect $\beta_1$ (adaptability) and $\beta_2$ (closeness) to be positive, and $\beta_3$ (intergenerational authority) and $\beta_4$ (history of family bonds) to be negative.

### 4.3 Robustness Checks

Based on the available text, specific robustness checks are not detailed. The paper's primary design choice to control for confounders is the restriction to a single industry with held-constant family influence. Additional robustness analyses (e.g., alternative model specifications, alternative variable operationalizations, sensitivity analyses) are not described in the extracted text and would require access to the full methodology and results sections.

### 4.4 Mechanism and Heterogeneity

- **Mechanism:** The paper does not test a causal mechanism in the traditional mediation sense. Rather, it theorizes that socioemotional wealth dimensions shape the owner-manager's *willingness* to innovate during succession — a perception-based channel. The "Ability and Willingness Paradox" framework posits that while family firms have concentrated ability to innovate (via ownership control), their willingness is driven by non-economic/socioemotional factors. The paper tests which specific SEW dimensions are associated with this willingness but does not formally decompose the pathway.

- **Heterogeneity:** The paper's central contribution is precisely about heterogeneity — showing that different SEW dimensions have opposing effects on innovation willingness. However, the paper does not systematically test heterogeneous treatment effects across subgroups (e.g., by firm size, generation, or region) in the available text. The heterogeneity documented is in the *direction* of SEW effects rather than in subgroup-specific magnitudes.
